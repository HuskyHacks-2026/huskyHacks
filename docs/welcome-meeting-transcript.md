It really boils down to two productivities / two problem types that we’re seeing in cases with, and it’s called account takeovers, and the other one is imposter scams. So to give you a little bit of overview of the difference between these two:

**Imposter scams** are when a member is deceived — either through trust, fear, some type of emotion — and they are the ones conducting the transaction or the transfer, and so they’re authorizing it. Crazy — a lot of these when it comes to romance scams, defense [? / “defense” / “elder”] scams…

**Host:** Can you tell us about what you’re currently working on to help protect our members from being taken advantage?

**Dallas [?]**  
Yeah — right now we’re looking at a lot of different [? carriers / channels] here… credit scores and Zelle, wires… we’re just trying right now [to address] account takeover money transfers. **Charlene — losses / friction tradeoff:**  
So, imposter scams — because the member is the one conducting the transaction and pushing the button — any losses that are incurred there are the member’s losses, so the credit union itself is not the one that’s taking the losses in that scenario, which we’re seeing really large volumes of.

We see a lot more account takeovers with lower dollar amounts because we have limits. Since, as Diana [? / Dallas] said, members are able to look into their account activity and notice $200 gone, notice a thousand dollars gone, and report that immediately. If they’re reporting it immediately in a takeover situation, they are credited, and it’s the credit union that takes the loss.

So right now, I know in 2025 that was about a $300 million loss for institutions that reported across the nation — not everyone’s reporting those, so it’s understated for sure. But when it comes to imposter scams, those are member losses, not credit union losses. But to Jen’s point: our members are shareholders, right? So we take that just as seriously as we do credit union losses. They also look to us as experts, so it’s a difficult position to be in, right? You want the service, and you want it fast. And if I put friction in because I think [it’s a scam] — yeah, the member gets upset. In that situation they want the transaction fast and timely. If it *is* a scam, we see them come back and they are upset with us because we did *not* put enough friction — because now they are out tens of thousands of dollars.

## Highest-signal takeaways for building

1. **Problem:** stop scams **before money moves** — authorized/imposter scams *and/or* account takeover; prevention + real-time intervention + recovery/support.
2. **Don’t rebuild** existing digital banking; **add on** what they don’t have for *this* problem.
3. **Make it personal** — member story / loved one; simple, intuitive UX or it gets buried like alerts.
4. **Think like the fraudster** (bypass paths) *and* the member (friction vs. regret).
5. **Scale + interoperability** (APIs / MCPs); clear persona; don’t boil the ocean.
6. **Demo that clicks** — show the journey, false positives, override, pilot metrics; best ideas don’t need a sales pitch.
7. **Use only synthetic / provided data** — no biometrics, device intel, carrier data, consortium feeds, or personal communications assumed.
8. **Ask white shirts (judges) and purple shirts (Sound)** — redirect early if you’re on a bad path.
